# Which Debt Should I Pay Off First?

When you have several debts, deciding to pay extra is only half the decision. You still need to answer a more practical question:

**Which balance should get the next extra dollar?**

There are two common answers. The **Debt Snowball** method starts with your smallest balance. The **Debt Avalanche** method starts with your highest interest rate. A custom order can also make sense when a deadline, shared household priority, or other real-life constraint matters more than either default.

The right order is the one that fits your numbers, protects your essential cash flow, and gives you a plan you can keep following.

## The short answer

If quick wins help you stay motivated, consider paying the **smallest balance first** with the Snowball method.

If minimizing interest is your top priority, consider paying the **highest-APR balance first** with the Avalanche method.

In either case:

1. Keep making at least the required payment on every debt.
2. Choose one focus debt for your extra payment.
3. Recheck the plan when a balance, rate, or monthly budget changes.

Do not choose a method from a slogan alone. Compare both using your actual balances, APRs, minimum payments, and realistic extra-payment amount.

## Start with a safe monthly amount

Before choosing the first debt, decide how much you can consistently put toward debt each month.

Start with:

- Monthly take-home income
- Essential expenses
- Minimum debt payments
- A reasonable cash buffer
- The extra amount left after those obligations

Paying aggressively can feel productive, but a plan that leaves no room for groceries, transportation, medical needs, or an unexpected bill is fragile. If the extra amount changes from month to month, use a conservative baseline and update the plan when more cash is available.

If you currently cannot pay more than the minimums, that is not a failure. Staying current while stabilizing your budget can be the right next step. The payoff order becomes useful when there is extra money to direct.

## Option 1: Pay the smallest balance first

The Debt Snowball method orders debts from the smallest balance to the largest balance. Interest rate does not determine the order.

You make the required payment on every debt, then direct the extra amount to the smallest balance. After that balance reaches zero, its old payment and your extra amount roll into the next debt.

### Why people choose Snowball

The first payoff can arrive sooner because the starting balance is smaller. That creates a visible win and removes one payment from the list.

Snowball can be useful when:

- You feel overwhelmed by the number of accounts.
- You have struggled to stay consistent with longer plans.
- Closing one balance would make the month easier to manage.
- Visible progress matters more to you than mathematical optimization.

### The tradeoff

The smallest debt may not have the highest APR. If a larger balance has a much higher rate, prioritizing the smallest balance can cost more interest than an Avalanche plan under the same payment assumptions.

That does not make Snowball irrational. Consistency has value. It does mean you should see the tradeoff before deciding.

## Option 2: Pay the highest APR first

The Debt Avalanche method orders debts from the highest interest rate to the lowest interest rate.

You make the required payment on every debt, then direct the extra amount to the highest-APR balance. When it is paid off, you roll that payment into the debt with the next-highest APR.

### Why people choose Avalanche

APR is the price of carrying a balance. Starting with the highest rate attacks the most expensive debt first.

Avalanche can be useful when:

- Reducing total interest is your main objective.
- You are comfortable waiting longer for the first account to close.
- Your highest-rate balance is materially more expensive than the others.
- A math-first plan helps you stay confident and consistent.

### The tradeoff

The highest-APR debt may also have a large balance. It can take longer to see the first account disappear, even while the plan is working.

If slow visible progress makes you abandon the plan or frequently switch priorities, the theoretical interest advantage may not translate into a better real-world result.

## A simple example

Imagine three debts:

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum payment |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Store Card | $650 | 24% | $35 |
| Credit Card | $3,200 | 29% | $110 |
| Auto Loan | $7,800 | 7% | $245 |

With Snowball, the order begins with the **$650 Store Card** because it has the smallest balance. The Credit Card comes next, followed by the Auto Loan.

With Avalanche, the order begins with the **29% Credit Card** because it has the highest APR. The Store Card comes next, followed by the Auto Loan.

The same debts produce two different first actions:

- Snowball says: get the smaller Store Card to zero first.
- Avalanche says: focus on the more expensive Credit Card first.

You need a payoff calculation to compare the projected debt-free date and interest under each method. The best choice is not visible from balances alone.

## When a custom payoff order makes sense

Real life does not always fit a two-method comparison. A custom order may be more useful when:

- A promotional APR will expire soon.
- A family loan carries an important relationship commitment.
- A small balance is creating disproportionate administrative stress.
- A secured debt has a specific risk or deadline.
- A household partner needs a shared priority to stay aligned.

Custom does not mean random. Write down the reason for the exception, decide how long it applies, and review the plan after that condition changes.

## Four questions to choose your method

### 1. What keeps you following a plan?

Look at your past behavior without judging it. If you lose momentum when results feel distant, an earlier payoff win may be valuable. If a clear mathematical rule reduces decision fatigue, Avalanche may feel steadier.

### 2. How different are the APRs?

If the rates are close, the interest difference between methods may be modest. If one rate is far higher, the Avalanche case becomes stronger. Run the full schedule rather than guessing from the percentages.

### 3. How long until the first payoff?

Compare the projected month of the first paid-off account under each method. That date helps you understand the motivational tradeoff in concrete terms.

### 4. Can your budget support the plan?

A payoff strategy cannot fix a monthly shortfall. If required payments and essentials already use all available income, focus first on stability, due dates, and avoiding new balances. Consider a qualified nonprofit credit counselor if you need individualized help.

## Common mistakes to avoid

### Switching methods every month

Changing direction after every statement can scatter extra payments across several balances. Choose a method, document the reason, and change it only when your numbers or priorities materially change.

### Ignoring minimum payments

The focus debt receives the extra payment. Other debts still need their required payments. Missing those can create fees, credit damage, or other consequences.

### Using an unrealistic extra-payment amount

A plan based on a perfect month can break the first time an irregular expense appears. Use an amount that works in ordinary months, then treat additional money as an optional acceleration.

### Looking only at the final date

The projected debt-free date matters, but so do the path and the first milestone. Review the order, monthly payment demand, first payoff, and interest estimate together.

## How to build your payoff order

Gather these details for each debt:

- Current balance
- APR
- Required minimum payment
- Due date
- Any promotional-rate expiration or special condition

Then:

1. Enter the debts into a payoff calculator.
2. Set a realistic monthly extra payment.
3. Run the Snowball method.
4. Run the Avalanche method.
5. Compare payoff order, first payoff, projected debt-free date, and interest.
6. Choose one method for the next review period.
7. Update balances regularly so the projection stays useful.

The goal is not to find a perfect plan that never changes. It is to maintain a clear plan that can adapt without forcing you to start over.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I pay the highest balance first?

Usually, balance size alone is not the decision rule. Snowball uses the smallest balance, while Avalanche uses the highest APR. A high balance may come later under either method unless another constraint makes it a custom priority.

### Should I pay off a credit card before a loan?

It depends on the balances, APRs, minimum payments, and any special loan terms. Credit cards often have higher rates, but compare the actual accounts rather than relying only on debt type.

### Is Snowball or Avalanche faster?

With the same monthly payment, the answer depends on your debt mix. Avalanche generally prioritizes interest efficiency; Snowball prioritizes earlier balance closures. Calculate both schedules for your numbers.

### Can I combine the two methods?

Yes. For example, you might pay off one very small balance for momentum and then switch to the highest APR. Treat that as a deliberate custom plan and calculate the resulting schedule so the tradeoff is visible.

### How often should I update my plan?

Update it when balances, rates, minimum payments, or your available extra amount change. A monthly check-in is a practical rhythm for many households.
